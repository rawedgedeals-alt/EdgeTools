Your small business software stack: what to keep, cut, and add

Before buying another app, do a 30-minute audit of the tools you already pay for. The goal isn't to collect the most software. It's to cover your essential work with fewer surprises and fewer overlapping bills.

Updated: October 27, 2026 · Read time: about 7 minutes

Start with the jobs, not the brand names

Write down the actual work your business does every week. Most small operations need a way to handle a few of these jobs, not a separate paid subscription for every one:

Customer messagesSchedulingInvoices & paymentsWebsite / storefrontFile storageMarketingCollaborationAutomations

Some businesses also have industry-specific compliance, inventory, security or recordkeeping needs. Those come before convenience features.

Follow this six-step audit

1. Inventory every recurring charge

Look at statements and software account billing pages. Record the product, owner, monthly or annual fee, renewal date, number of paid seats and what task it supports. Include fees that do not appear every month, like annual domains, add-ons, per-user charges and variable usage.

Tip: don't record passwords, bank credentials or full payment details in the worksheet.

2. Mark the tools that actually get used

For every tool, ask: Who used it in the last 30 days? What work would stop if it disappeared tomorrow? Can another tool already on the list do the same job? Keep a tool because it solves a real task, not because a discount or trial made it sound useful.

3. Find duplicate features

Typical overlap areas include scheduling, email campaigns, file storage, design, task management, website forms and AI assistants. Two similar services can be worthwhile when they do different work, but first identify exactly what the second subscription adds.

4. Calculate full first-year cost

A monthly headline price isn't the whole cost. Add base fees, seats, onboarding, integrations, transaction charges where applicable, and any usage you can reasonably estimate. Account for the price after a promotion ends. Use our free software cost calculator for a baseline estimate, then add variable expenses separately.

5. Test one replacement on real work

Choose one repeatable example, such as capturing a lead, sending a confirmation or organizing a customer request. Run the same task in both products. Check errors, effort, available integrations, access permissions, support documentation and what happens when you need to export your data. Don't migrate an entire business based on a demo video.

6. Set a review date

Review the stack before major renewals or whenever the team and workflow changes. Recheck pricing and plan limits with the provider, because software offers and features can change. Your final decision should be one of four labels: Keep, Cancel after export, Consolidate, or Test before deciding.

Which kind of tool should you compare first?

Need a website or online shop? Start by deciding whether checkout and product inventory are essential. Compare Shopify vs Wix, Shopify vs Webflow, and our small-business Shopify guide. Provider websites are the source of current prices and terms.

Repeating a lot of manual steps? Map the steps first, then read Zapier vs Make. Check the specific triggers, actions, credits or tasks your workflow would consume before estimating costs.

Making content or doing design work? Read Canva vs Figma to decide whether you mostly need quick published assets or an interface-design collaboration workflow.

Considering AI assistants? Evaluate them on a few real tasks using the same inputs, and review data-handling settings before uploading confidential material. Our ChatGPT vs Claude guide provides a practical comparison.

Three questions that prevent expensive software mistakes

  1. Can I leave? Verify export options, cancellation terms, migration effort, and how long data remains available.
  2. Who can access it? Confirm account permissions, MFA options and what information employees or contractors can see.
  3. What happens when the trial ends? Check plan limits, billing cadence, renewal terms, and whether features you need require extra add-ons.

The bottom line

A good software stack isn't the one with the most logos. It's the smallest set of tools that reliably handles your real work, with sensible security and a cost you can explain. Audit first, compare second, subscribe last.

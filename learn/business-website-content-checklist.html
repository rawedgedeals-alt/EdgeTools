What to Put on a Small Business Website Before You Launch

A simple checklist of the business details, customer questions and contact information that make a one-page website useful.

Published 2026-11-03 · Independent educational guide

Make the first screen answer a real question

A visitor should be able to tell what your business does, who it serves and how to take the next step without hunting through several menus. Start with your business name, a plain-language description and one primary action. If you offer local services, clarify the service area rather than listing every nearby city for search engines.

Imagine explaining your business to someone who has never met you. Replace general claims such as 'the best solutions' with specific information about the service you offer. Clear writing often improves a small page more than extra effects or complicated design.

Give people enough information to decide

Answer the common practical questions: what is offered, how it works, approximate service boundaries, hours or response expectations, and who the service is for. Add trustworthy examples only when you have permission and can verify them. Avoid fabricated customer reviews or guarantees.

Photos can help, but they should be relevant, licensed for your use and readable on a phone. A lightweight page with clear headings and sufficient contrast is often easier to use than a dramatic page that hides the details.

Make contact simple and safe

Choose a contact path that you really monitor: phone, business email, booking link or physical location. Do not promise immediate replies if that is not realistic. If you collect information through a form, explain what you need and how it will be handled; avoid collecting sensitive details without a reason.

Check every link and call-to-action on a phone. Confirm that tapping a number starts the expected phone action and that any email address is correct. Simple usability checks prevent avoidable lost inquiries.

Understand the difference between a file and a hosted site

A downloadable HTML file is a real website document, but it is not automatically visible at a public domain. Going live normally involves hosting, a domain or subdomain, and checking how the page behaves online. Search visibility can take additional time and is not guaranteed.
